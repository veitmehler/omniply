## Post 1 — the empty elevator (Wed Sep 9)

Elevators used to have drivers.

For half a century, nobody would ride without one. Push-button elevators
existed by 1900. People stepped in, saw no operator, and stepped right
back out.

But in 1945, New York's elevator operators went on strike. Because the city
bled millions a day, buildings installed the buttons. Riders
grumbled, then adjusted, then forgot there had ever been a stool in the
corner.

Nobody went on strike against practice marketing. But I remember the
moment I realized the operator's seat was emptying. 

On December 10th, 2022, a few days after ChatGPT came out, I played
around with it to code a simple marketing WordPress plugin for a client. 

I was amazed by how fast it wrote the code... unbelievable, and that was 
version 1! 

When I spoke to that longtime client later that day, I told him that
this wouldn't just help with his clinic marketing, it would take over the
entire process... end to end, while he as the owner would just have to 
click a few buttons.

He told me, "No way! Look how much you have to debug. Why don't you just
write the plugin yourself."

Fair enough. At the time, the code was still full of bugs, but you could
already see the speed the lines of code came out. No human could type that
fast.

Here's the thing about early adoption: the automatic elevator was safe for
forty-five years before anyone trusted it. Timing was never about the
technology. It's about the moment people decide to step in and push the
button themselves.

I think that moment is now. This week I'll show you why.

Which operator's seat is emptying in your industry right now?


## Post 2 — the photographer question (Thursday Sep 10)

When cameras went digital, photographers were supposed to disappear.

Film cost money. Every click was a decision. Then photos became free,
infinite, disposable. Everyone owned a camera. Everyone was a
photographer.

And yet: weddings still hire one.

Because the scarce thing was never the shutter. It was knowing where to
stand. How to compose the frame. How to capture the right moment without
having to delete 200 photos where someone has their eyes closed...

Seeing the moment forming two seconds before it arrives. The ability to
pick the right spot, the perfect composition, being trusted in the room
on the biggest day of someone's life.

That's the true value of a great photographer.

Software is having its digital-camera moment. 

You see, that plugin from my last post? What took a dev team weeks in 2020 
now gets done in an afternoon. 

Working software has become almost free.

So I started asking the photographer question about local practices:
when production is free, what still costs something?

Three answers kept coming back.

- Your voice. Patients spot generic content the way you spot a stock
photo.
- Your evenings. A tool that adds review work steals the very thing it
promised to give back.
- Your license. In healthcare, one sloppy claim outweighs a thousand good
posts.

None of those are production problems. They are trust problems. And
trust doesn't scale by shutter clicks.

The system I built starts from that premise. Tomorrow, I'll show you
its portfolio.


## Post 3 — never trust a skinny chef (Friday Sep 11)

For the past month, a machine has been publishing under my name. With my
permission.

Every article at omniply.io/articles: researched, written, illustrated,
distributed, announced. All of it produced by an engine I built. My only
job is the last look before anything ships.

I run my own marketing this way for the same reason you should never
trust a skinny chef. If I'm asking practice owners to let a system cook
in their kitchen, in their voice, under their name, then I eat here
first. Every day. In public.

So judge the kitchen by the plates. 

Last week I had to reject a headline because it promised too much and
sounded like cookie-cutter AI. 

That type of vetoing is my job now, not writing from an empty screen.

Because the uncomfortable truth: it markets my business better than I ever did.

Not because it's brilliant. Because it never skips a day, and I always
did.

Consistency was never a talent problem. It was a capacity problem. That
is what actually changed.

Monday, I'll show you the one thing I built it to never do.


## Post 4 — the captain's signature (Monday Sep 14)

Autopilot has flown most of every flight you've ever taken.

It holds altitude better than any human. It doesn't get tired over the
Atlantic. And at the front of the plane there is still, always, a
captain.

Not because the technology needs babysitting. Because the flight needs
someone accountable. A signature. A person whose judgment, and whose
license, stand behind the machine's precision.

That's the design I stole for practice marketing.

My engine researches, writes, designs, schedules, distributes. It could
publish on its own. I built it so it can't. 

Nothing reaches the public until the owner looks at it and presses approve. 

Five minutes, coffee in hand. 

The machine does the eighty percent that eats your evenings. You do the twenty 
percent only a licensed professional can: judge... the output quality.

In healthcare this isn't philosophy, it's survival. No promise of outcome... 
no invented patient stories... no claims your license can't carry. 

An AI publishing unsupervised in this space isn't a productivity win. It's a
liability with a monthly fee.

Precision from the machine. Accountability from the captain.

Tomorrow morning, the doors open.
